The report is not an application for assistance, but it's an important step toward city-wide aid. It's for flooded homes, not outbuildings, trees, or fences.

When the water leaves, most people think the hard part is over. In our experience it's the opposite. The standing water was the visible problem. What it left behind, in wall cavities, subfloors, crawlspaces, and wiring, is the one that costs real money, and it keeps getting worse the longer it sits. Flood recovery happens in two stages, and it helps to know which is which.

Mitigation comes first and is time-sensitive: pumping out water, removing wet materials, running drying equipment, and stopping mold before it starts. ## Why flooding here is its own problem

Northern Arizona isn't known for flooding, but the damage is real and it comes from several directions.

Monsoon storms drop a lot of rain in a short window onto ground that can't absorb it. Neighborhoods below the Museum, Pipeline, and Tunnel fire burn scars see runoff and debris flows that the land used to hold back. Spring snowmelt pushes water against foundations and into crawlspaces. And once temperatures drop, water already inside a wall or slab freezes, expands, and cracks what it was sitting in.

Our older housing stock makes it worse. Many Flagstaff homes have crawlspaces, wood sill plates sitting on or near grade, and original wiring and plumbing. Water reaches the structural wood and stays there.

## What floodwater actually damages

The carpet and drywall you can see are the cheap part. The expensive damage tends to be hidden:

- Sill plates and joists that stay wet absorb moisture and begin to rot, often with no visible sign for months
- Insulation, which holds water and loses its value as insulation
- Subfloor under finished flooring, where moisture lingers after the surface feels dry
- Drywall, which wicks water several inches above the visible waterline
- Outlets, wiring, and panels that were wet or submerged
- Foundation walls and slabs, where saturated soil can shift and crack concrete

A moisture meter and an open wall tell you what a visual walkthrough can't. That's why our first step on any water job is an assessment with readings and photos.

## The insurance problem

Most homeowners find out what their policy covers after the loss, and it's often less than they assumed. These are the places claims run into trouble.

Standard homeowners policies generally exclude flood, meaning rising or surface water from rain, runoff, or snowmelt. Sudden internal events like a burst pipe are usually covered. The same wet floor can fall on either side of that line depending on where the water came from, and that distinction is where many disputes start.

Flood coverage is a separate policy, through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private insurer, and NFIP policies generally carry a 30-day waiting period before they take effect. If you live near a burn scar and don't have a policy, buying one after the next storm warning is too late. Sewer and drain backup is another separate endorsement that many policies don't include.

Scope disputes are the other common problem. The adjuster's estimate is often based on visible damage and misses wet insulation, subfloor, and framing inside the walls. A homeowner who gets a low estimate and a contractor's much higher one is in a real disagreement, and the better documented side tends to win it. Insurers can also deny part of a claim as long-term seepage or deferred maintenance, so photos of the home's condition before the loss help.

Your policy requires you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Waiting on repairs while you argue about the claim can reduce what's owed, because the insurer can say the added damage was avoidable. Keep receipts for mitigation, and ask your insurer in writing what they require before work starts.

Finally, if your home is in a mapped floodplain and the damage is significant, local floodplain rules may require the repair to meet current standards, such as elevating utilities. NFIP policies include Increased Cost of Compliance coverage that can help pay for this, and few owners know it exists. Your local floodplain administrator can tell you whether the rule applies to you.

We aren't public adjusters and we don't negotiate your claim for you. What we do is write a line-item repair scope with photos and moisture readings, which is the documentation you need if you ask the insurer to reinspect or adjust the estimate. ## Top reasons to repair now

1. **Mold doesn't wait.** It can begin growing within 24 to 48 hours of a wetting, and it spreads inside wall cavities and under flooring where you can't see it.
2. **Wet framing loses strength.** Sill plates, joists, and studs that stay damp rot slowly and quietly, and replacing structural members costs far more than drying them in time.
3. **Wiring and panels are a fire and shock risk.** Water in outlets, junction boxes, or a panel should be inspected and repaired before anyone relies on that circuit again.
4. **Insurance coverage can shrink.** Delay hands the insurer an argument that additional damage was preventable.
5. **The bill grows.** A job that starts as wet drywall and insulation becomes a framing, subfloor, and flooring job if it's left, and the cost climbs with each step.
6. **Winter finishes what the flood started.** Moisture left in a wall or slab freezes and expands through freeze-thaw cycles, cracking things that would otherwise have survived.
7. **It follows the house at sale.** Unrepaired water history turns up in inspections, and lenders and buyers react to it.
8. **Your family breathes it.** Mold and damp materials affect air quality, and older residents and children are most sensitive.

## What to do in the first week

Take photos and video of everything before anything is moved or thrown out, including the waterline, damaged belongings, and the surrounding area. Call your insurer and get the claim number in writing. Hire a licensed mitigation company right away and keep their moisture logs. Don't throw out damaged materials until the adjuster has seen them or you've documented them well. ## Common questions

**Does homeowners insurance cover flood damage?** Usually not. Standard policies exclude flood from rising or surface water but typically cover sudden internal water events such as a burst pipe. Check your declarations page and ask your agent about flood and sewer backup coverage.

**How long do I have before mold sets in?** Mold can begin growing within 24 to 48 hours in wet, enclosed materials. Drying fast is the best protection.

**Can you work while my claim is still open?** Yes. We can assess and document the damage before the claim is settled, and we won't start repairs that would destroy evidence the adjuster needs without your insurer's go-ahead.

**Will you work directly with my adjuster?** We'll send our scope, photos, and moisture readings, and answer technical questions from the adjuster. The claim itself stays between you and your insurer.

**Do repairs need permits?** Structural, electrical, and plumbing repairs generally do, and significant work in a floodplain may trigger extra requirements. We tell you what applies and pull the permits.
